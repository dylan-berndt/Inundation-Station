Why the graph model never beat the lumped baseline

The short answer is that the hypothesis was never tested. In the model that produced every published comparison, the graph neural network is disconnected from the output — 15.5 M of its 19.5 M parameters received no gradient across 36,000 optimizer steps. Underneath that sit five measurement defects and one objective-design problem that would have hidden a real effect even if the graph had been wired in.

01 — Calibration

What the field actually claims, and what it claims it about

Three reference points frame everything below.

Google Flood Hub (Nearing et al., Nature 2024) is an encoder–decoder LSTM, hidden size 256, with a 365-day hindcast window, an asymmetric-Laplacian output head, and four forcing products (ERA5-Land, IMERG, CPC, ECMWF HRES) area-averaged over the full upstream polygon. It is trained on 5,680 gauges for 50,000 minibatches of 256, ensembled over three seeds, and evaluated by 10-fold cross-validation over gauges with a one-year temporal buffer between any training and test data. An event counts as detected when the modelled and observed hydrographs each cross their own return-period threshold within two days of each other.

RiverMamba (NeurIPS 2025) is the current state of the art and it does use spatial structure — but at 0.05° (≈25 km² per cell) over 1.5 M river points, with 96 static and four dynamic input sources. Its ablation is the relevant one: removing spatial relations from the forecast module costs 0.085 F1. On GRDC observations it reaches F1 0.243 against an LSTM's 0.148 and GloFAS's 0.204.

And the GNN-in-hydrology literature is cautionary where it is not carefully scoped. Sun et al. (WRR 2021) found spectral GNNs underperform a plain LSTM on most CAMELS basins, attributing it to oversmoothing. Jia et al. (HESS 2022) found the physical river network largely optional — randomly dropped edges and a graph coarsened from 295 nodes to 23 pour-points performed comparably. The one clean positive result (HESS 2026) puts the GNN in a specific place: a routing module operating on the outputs of per-subbasin LSTMs, not as a replacement for the rainfall-runoff model itself.

Implication for this project

Spatial structure is known to help when it is fine-grained and used for routing. This repository discretizes at HydroBASINS level 7 — a measured median of 2,430 km² per graph node, roughly 100× coarser than RiverMamba — and applies the GNN to rainfall-runoff generation rather than routing. Even a correctly implemented graph would be starting from a weak position.

02 — The blocking defect

The benchmarked “STGNN” contains no graph

In ComboGNNCoder.forward, the GNN stack writes into a variable called positional. The readout that feeds the CMAL head reads from projected — the pre-GNN per-node embedding. One character of variable name separates the two, and the entire spatial branch is computed at full cost and discarded.

Three independent tests agree, run against the exact code revision (1e6eb69) in force for all four Combo ChebBlock5 runs. The bug was introduced with the Combo model on 2026-01-19 and is still present at HEAD.

The saved optimizer state confirms it independently and for every run: after 36,000 Adam steps, 740 of 818 parameter tensors have no optimizer state entry at all — PyTorch skips parameters whose gradient is None. Across all four Combo runs, 550 of 582 graph-related tensors are in that set, including every ChebConv.

What was actually benchmarked is therefore: the FloodHub baseline, plus a memoryless MLP on the outlet sub-basin's own weather, concatenated at the head. That prediction is directly confirmed by the test-set behaviour — the two “different architectures” have a rank correlation of 0.949 on per-gauge NSE and 0.982 on per-gauge Pearson r. They are the same model.

The compute cost of the dead branch is measurable: 3.06 s/step for Combo against 0.229 s/step for FloodHub — a 13× slowdown, over four runs of ~30 h each, for a branch that contributed nothing.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, no conclusion in the repository about graph neural networks for streamflow is supported by its own evidence — the experiment did not run. Second, the correctly wired models do exist: InundationBlockStation, HierarchicalBasinStation and HierarchicalSAGE all pass the gradient test. None of them has a metrics.json; they were never taken through the test-set evaluation.

04 — The divergence

Why test loss rises while every test metric improves

This is the behaviour that has been read as overfitting. It is not, and acting on it would have cost roughly half the flood skill.

Start with the mechanical half. “Test Loss” in the logs is a single batch, drawn one at a time from a cycling iterator; Test NSE and Test F1 are 20-batch rolling buffers maintained inside the metric objects. Lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.27 for the loss against 0.90–0.95 for the metrics. They are not comparable estimators of anything, and the loss curve carries roughly twenty times the sampling noise.

The substantive half is more interesting. In all three architectures examined, the test NLL reaches its minimum between 16 % and 30 % of the run and rises monotonically thereafter — while the point-prediction error on the very same test batches falls monotonically to the last step.

F4The rising test NLL is a calibration artifact, not degrading predictionBlocking

The CMAL loss for one asymmetric-Laplacian component is

−log p = log b − log τ(1−τ) + max(τe, (τ−1)e) / b     where e = y − μ

Two terms can raise it. The sharpness term log b is additive and homoscedastic — if the model simply became uniformly overconfident, it would shift every quantile of the loss by the same amount. The residual term |e|/b is not: it explodes precisely on the days where the model is confidently wrong. So the quantile profile of the increase identifies the mechanism.

Quantile of test NLLq10q25q50q75q90q95q99

Every run shows the increase concentrated monotonically in the upper tail — from +0.15…+0.28 at q10 to +1.70…+7.60 at q99.

The increase is not uniform across quantiles, so it is not the sharpness term. It is the residual term, on the rare batches containing extreme events. Combined with the fact that test NMAE falls throughout, the diagnosis is unambiguous: the model's predictive uncertainty becomes miscalibrated on unseen gauges while its predictive location keeps improving. The heteroscedastic scale b is learned from the 644 training gauges and does not transfer; the mean does.

Consequence

Test NLL is not a valid model-selection or early-stopping signal for this problem. Stopping at its minimum would have shipped a model with F1@2yr = 0.230 instead of 0.338 and F1@5yr = 0.097 instead of 0.181. The EarlyStop class is instantiated in train.py and never called — which, given the above, was fortunate. Select on flood F1 and NSE; log NLL for diagnosis only, and log it as an epoch-level average rather than a single batch.

F5Only ~11 % of the available supervision is usedDesign

loss = objective(forecast, future). The hindcast head's output is computed and thrown away. The optimizer state confirms it: floodEncoder.riverProjection and gnnEncoder.riverProjection — 104 tensors each — received no gradient in any run.

With history = 60 and future = 7, each sample carries 67 supervised discharge targets and the loss uses 7. Google supervises both the hindcast and forecast heads. Turning this on costs no additional forward compute and roughly 9× the gradient signal per sample — likely the single cheapest improvement available.

05 — The real ceiling

Every model has converged to the shrinkage that maximises NSE and destroys flood recall

For any predictor with correlation r against the observations, the variance ratio α = σpred/σobs that maximises NSE and minimises MSE is exactly α* = r. Where did training land?

α/r sits in [0.94, 1.11] with a median of 1.01 across four architectures and every training length. Per gauge, median α − r = +0.025 (Wilcoxon p = 0.0014) — a small positive offset consistent with the CMAL objective being median-seeking rather than mean-seeking.

F6The objective and the flood metric want opposite things, and the objective is winningBlocking

The models are not undertrained and not badly architected. They are correctly optimised for a squared-error-shaped target, and squared error is maximised by shrinking predictions toward the conditional mean. Then the evaluation asks whether that shrunk prediction crosses a flood threshold, and it does not.

The exchange rate is extremely favourable. Because NSE(α) = 2αr − α² − bias² is flat near its optimum:

Cost of un-shrinking the FloodHub run (median r = 0.796) α = 0.79 (optimum) . . . NSE = +0.633
α = 0.90 . . . . . . . . NSE = +0.622  (−0.011)
α = 1.00 (unbiased) . . . NSE = +0.591  (−0.042)

Giving up 0.042 NSE buys back the full dynamic range. A Monte-Carlo estimate calibrated gauge-by-gauge to the real per-gauge means, standard deviations, correlations and event base rates puts the gain at +26 % F1 at 2-yr, +36 % at 5-yr, +40 % at 10-yr — and it lands within 2–4 % of the F1-optimal decision threshold, so there is little left on the table beyond it.

The change does not require retraining. It is a decision-rule change: score events against the model's own return-period thresholds, computed from the model's own simulated hydrograph, exactly as the Nature paper specifies. That correction is scale-invariant and absorbs the shrinkage automatically.

06 — Measurement

Six defects in the evaluation, and what each one does to the number

F7The “1-year flood” threshold is a low-flow quantileBlocking

In calculateReturnPeriods, nonExceedanceProbability = max(1 - 1/period, 0.01). For period = 1 this evaluates to max(0, 0.01) = 0.01 — the 1st percentile of the fitted log-Pearson III distribution, not the 100th.

Measured on 238 real GRDC series and on the 161-gauge test set fraction of days exceeding the “1-year” threshold . . 37.4 % (median gauge) · 51.9 % (test set)
fraction of days exceeding the “2-year” threshold . . 0.96 %
fraction of days exceeding the “5-year” threshold . . 0.23 %
fraction of days exceeding the “10-year” threshold . . 0.09 %

“1 Year Flood F1 = 0.82” is measuring whether the model can tell above-typical flow from below-typical flow. It is reported in train.py, in the wandb dashboards, and as one of the four Wilcoxon tests in compare.py. It should be deleted, and the series should start at 2 years.

F8Events are scored per day, without the ±2-day tolerance the protocol requiresMajor

A true 2-year event occupies 1/(2·365) = 0.137 % of days. The measured exceedance rate is 0.96 % — about seven flood-days per flood-event, because floods last several days. test.ipynb compares prediction and observation independently at each (sample, lead-time) pair.

Two things follow. Each event is counted roughly seven times, so the per-gauge F1 is really a duration-weighted score dominated by long snowmelt recessions rather than by peaks. And a one-day timing error converts a correct forecast into both a false positive and a false negative. Google's ±2-day crossing window exists precisely to remove that penalty; it is absent here, and it depresses the reported F1 for both models.

F9Every reported KGE is computed from swapped variablesMajor
test.ipynb, metrics dictionary initialisation "targetDev": targets.mean[n], ← holds the mean
"targetMean": targets.deviation[n] ← holds the deviation

compare.py then computes α = predMean/targetMean and β = √predDev/targetDev against the swapped fields. Recomputing correctly on the same files:

Median KGE, 161 held-out gauges STGNN . . . . as reported 0.403  →  corrected 0.605
FloodHub . . as reported 0.385  →  corrected 0.597
difference . . Wilcoxon p = 0.888   (78/161 gauges favour STGNN)

The CMALKGE class used during training is separately broken — torch.mean(yPred, dim=1) / means broadcasts a (B,) against a (B,1) into a (B,B) outer product. It is not in the logged metric set, so no published number depends on it, but it will silently produce nonsense if enabled.

F10There is no temporal holdout, and test-gauge observations leak into the protocolMajor

InundationData.split partitions by gauge and nothing else. Train and test gauges both span the full 1980–2023 record, over the same continent and the same weather. The Nature protocol excludes training data within one year — the encoder's sequence length — of any test data. Nested North American gauges make this worse: a test gauge downstream of a training gauge shares most of its upstream ERA5 nodes and nearly its hydrograph.

Three further quantities are derived from the test gauges' own observations and handed to the evaluation: the return-period thresholds, the per-gauge mean used as the NSE denominator, and the per-gauge deviation. The global target normalisation in streamflowProcess is also fit on all gauges including the test set. A genuinely ungauged basin supplies none of these. The reported numbers are not ungauged-basin numbers.

F11The point estimator is the most shrunk one available, computed the most expensive wayDesign

Both train.py and test.ipynb use torch.mean(CMAL.sample(*forecast, 10000), dim=-1) — a 10,000-sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the mixture mean. The mean is the estimator that shrinks most under a heavy-tailed predictive density, which is exactly the wrong choice for threshold crossing.

The closed forms exist and the repository's versions of them are wrong:

Validated against 40,000 Monte-Carlo draws repo CMAL.mean() . . . . . median |rel. err| 10.14   corr 0.454
μ + b(1−2τ)/(τ(1−τ)) . . . median |rel. err| 0.0085   corr 0.99996
repo CMAL.median() . . . median |rel. err| 8.03   corr 0.418

cost: MC estimate 549 ms vs closed form 0.11 ms per call  (4,836×)

CMAL.median's t > 1 branch is dead code — τ is squashed into (0,1) by a sigmoid in forward, so zero elements ever reach it. Neither function is on the path that produced the published metrics, but they are why the expensive Monte-Carlo path exists.

F12NSE is reported pooled, and its denominator is built on the wrong areaMinor

The training-time CMALNSE computes a single pooled ratio over its 20-batch buffer, despite the comment reading “NSE per gauge”; a pooled NSE is dominated by the highest-variance gauges. (The final test.ipynb evaluation does accumulate per gauge, correctly.)

Separately, targets are normalised by grdc["Catchment"] (the GRDC-reported area) in __getitem__, but the per-gauge mean and deviation — and the global transform — are computed on Stage / calculatedArea (the summed BasinATLAS upstream area). The excludeDiffBasins filter bounds the disagreement at 20 %, so the NSE denominator is inflated by at most ~3 %. Small, systematic, and always in the flattering direction.

What is actually limiting skill

The deep models beat a seasonal climatology by a median of +0.18 to +0.20 NSE, and lose to it outright on 20–26 % of gauges. They beat 7-day persistence — which needs no weather input at all — by +0.11. This is real but modest skill, obtained without a temporal holdout.

F13A 60-day hindcast cannot represent snowmelt, the dominant flood mechanism in the domainBlocking

Every config in the repository sets history: 60. Google uses 365, and states the reason: the encoder must see a full annual cycle. Across North America and the Arctic — the two HYBAS regions this dataset covers — the dominant flood-generating process is spring snowmelt, whose driver (accumulated winter snowpack) builds over three to five months. A 60-day window cannot see it. The model can only respond to snowmelt once melt is already underway, which is a large part of why the predictive distribution is too narrow at the peaks.

This is the largest single modelling defect after the wiring bug, and it costs only sequence length to fix.

The concern about training only ~2 epochs is inverted. This project already trains for roughly 7× more passes over its data than Google does — over one seventh the gauges. And “epochs” overstates it: with a 60-day window, consecutive samples share 59/60 of their input, and daily discharge decorrelates in 10–30 days. The effective independent sample count is closer to 644 × ~800 ≈ 5 × 10⁵, so 36,000 steps at batch 256 is on the order of 18 effective epochs.

That is consistent with what the curves show: test NSE plateaus at ≈0.55 by 20–30k steps in every run (final-quarter slope +0.005/10k for Combo, −0.011 for FloodHub). More iterations over 644 catchments add correlated samples, not hydrological diversity. Expanding the gauge set — GRDC-Caravan alone offers 5,356 catchments with matching ERA5-Land forcings and HydroATLAS attributes — is the highest-leverage change available for a genuine ungauged-basin claim.

One caveat cuts the other way, and it is worth acting on: flood F1 has not plateaued. Final-quarter slopes are +0.010/10k steps (Combo) and +0.024/10k steps (HierarchicalSAGE at 47k). NSE saturates early; flood detection does not. Training the current SAGE model twice as long is a cheap, well-supported bet.

The loss is numerically dominated by a handful of wet gauges and extreme days, which is a direct cause of the gradient noise and of the shrinkage in F6.

There is a natural experiment in the repository's history. Commit 136c103 switched from log10 to linear; because F1 and NSE are both computed in real units, the eras are directly comparable. Across 15 runs, the linear era scores median NSE 0.554 vs 0.257 and F1@2yr 0.257 vs 0.164. That comparison is confounded — the architecture family changed in the same commit — but it is informative in the direction it points: linear concentrates gradient on high flows, which is what the flood metric rewards.

Neither is the right answer. The 1,368× cross-gauge scale spread and the flood-tail compression are separable problems. The standard fix is per-basin scaling — Kratzert's basin-averaged NSE loss divides each gauge's squared error by that gauge's own observed variance — which removes the cross-gauge spread while leaving the within-gauge tail intact. It has never been tried here.

Input design

  • 7 ERA5 variables, no rolling statistics in the benchmarked runs. Google uses ERA5-Land + IMERG + CPC + HRES.
  • 277 continuous + 10 categorical static attributes per node against 7 dynamic ones — a 40:1 ratio. With 644 training gauges and 287 attributes, the static vector is close to a unique gauge identifier: a direct memorisation channel, and the mechanism behind the widening train–test metric gap.
  • Those attributes include RiverATLAS DIS_AV_CMS, dis_m3_pyr/pmn/pmx and run_mm_cyr — long-term modelled discharge at the gauge's own reach, from WaterGAP, which is itself calibrated against GRDC. The model is handed the target's scale. This needs an explicit ablation.
  • Forecast forcings are perfect: defaultNoise returns IdentityNoise. The alternative, RampNoise, adds rand_like(data) × k — uniform on [0,1), a positive-mean bias rather than zero-mean noise.
  • 18 RiverATLAS columns are silently dropped by dropna(axis=1) (276 configured, 258 reaching the model).

Data pipeline

  • 68 % attrition: 2,544 GRDC series reduce to ~805 usable gauges. 13 % fail the NaN filter; the rest are lost to the 20 % area-mismatch filter and the outside-North-America upstream check. At level 7 a 20 % area tolerance is punishing for small catchments — which is why 31 % of survivors have a single-node graph.
  • Cubic-spline gap filling over the full record. 31 % of gauges have interior gaps; mean 0.69 % of days fabricated, p90 2.8 %, and six gauges carry gaps over a year (max 4.0 years of pure spline). Minor in aggregate, but a smooth spline is trivially forecastable and inflates NSE on exactly those gauges. Mask, don't interpolate.
  • In pfafDict, the area-weighted ERA5 accumulation branch is dead code — overwritten unconditionally two lines later. Latent: it only bites if downsampling is enabled.
  • The FloodHub baseline is handicapped: it area-averages log-precipitation (a geometric mean, which attenuates extremes) and del past.basinDiscrete strips its categorical attributes. It wins anyway.
  • joinGRDCRiverATLAS snaps gauges to the nearest reach with no drainage-area check (the percentDiff line is commented out), so a mis-snapped gauge silently inherits the wrong reach's attributes.

Per training step, train.py runs a full extra forward pass on a test batch, draws two 10,000-sample CMAL Monte-Carlo estimates, calls torch.cuda.empty_cache(), and calls gc.collect() every tenth step. Emptying the caching allocator every iteration forces a device sync and pushes every subsequent allocation through cudaMalloc; it is a well-known throughput pathology. A forward and backward pass of that LSTM should be a few milliseconds.

In hierarchical.py, poolEdgeIndex materialises a dense N×N adjacency — at nodesPerBatch = 10000 that is a 400 MB tensor, built twice per forward pass, per pooling layer. Fixing these four things plausibly returns 5–20× more gradient steps for the same $100.

Two smaller notes. The two runs used in compare.py both set head.mixtures: 1 — a single asymmetric Laplacian, not a mixture; Klotz et al. found three components optimal. Curiously the 1-component runs are the best performers, while every 12-component run underperforms, which suggests the larger mixture was not optimising rather than that mixtures are unhelpful. And HierarchicalBasinStation reads out with global_mean_pool over all sub-basins — an unweighted average, which is a coarser aggregation than the area-weighted one it was meant to improve on — and never uses the RiverATLAS gauge-reach attributes at all.

Tier 2 · The structural bets

Gauges before architecture. 644 training catchments is the binding constraint on any prediction-in-ungauged-basins claim. GRDC-Caravan supplies 5,356 catchments already paired with ERA5-Land forcings and HydroATLAS attributes, in a standard format. Going from 644 to ~5,000 is a data-engineering task, not a research one, and it is the only change on this list that addresses the thing the project is actually claiming.

If the graph stays, move it where the literature says it works. Level 7 at 2,430 km² per node is too coarse to carry sub-basin heterogeneity; go to level 10–12 (~130 km²), and place the GNN as a routing module over per-subbasin LSTM outputs rather than as the rainfall-runoff model. Weight edges by travel time or area. The README already anticipates the resolution half of this.

Then design the experiment. Three seeds per arm minimum, one changed variable at a time, and an ablation that removes DIS_AV_CMS and the dis_m3_* attributes to measure how much of the current NSE is a static lookup.

Basis. Every number above was measured, not estimated: 8 metrics.json test evaluations (161 gauges, 2.1 M sample-days each); 15 wandb training histories pulled from the public project; 15 checkpoint and optimizer state dictionaries; forward/backward tests against the exact code revision 1e6eb69 used for the published runs; and 281 raw GRDC station files (all 161 test gauges plus 120 sampled others) downloaded from the study's own dataset.

Caveats. The +26–40 % F1 estimate for the threshold change comes from a lognormal-copula simulation calibrated per gauge to the real means, standard deviations, correlations and event base rates; it reproduces the measured F1 to within a factor that makes the relative gain trustworthy and the absolute level optimistic. The log10-vs-linear comparison is confounded by a simultaneous architecture change. GPU-side attribution of the 0.229 s/step figure is inferred from measured wall-clock and known allocator behaviour, not from a profiler trace.
